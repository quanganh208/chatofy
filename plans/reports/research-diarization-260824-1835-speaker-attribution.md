## 2. Cross-lingual generalization to Vietnamese

- **VoxVietnam** (arXiv 2501.00328, Jan 2026): 187,980 utterances / 1,406 speakers / 261h, built to extend the existing Vietnam-Celeb dataset with multi-genre (broadcast+podcast+YouTube) data. Baseline (single-genre-trained) models get **14.17% EER (easy) / 22.69% EER (hard)** on Vietnamese test sets; multi-genre training brings it to **13.29% / 21.94%**. These are Vietnamese-native-trained models, not VoxCeleb-cross-lingual numbers — the paper does not report a direct "VoxCeleb-only model tested on Vietnamese" cross-lingual EER, so I cannot give you the exact degradation delta you asked for. **This is the one search result the literature does not answer cleanly** — treat as an open question.
- A separate result found in search snippets: fine-tuning a VoxCeleb2-pretrained ResNetSE-34 on Vietnamese data drops EER **5.98% → 2.22%** — implying the VoxCeleb-only (no fine-tune) baseline on Vietnamese sits somewhere around/above 6% EER, i.e. roughly an order of magnitude worse than the ~0.7% VoxCeleb1-O in-domain number. Take this as an order-of-magnitude signal, not a precise citation (secondary source, not the original paper directly fetched).
- General cross-lingual SV literature (arXiv 2007.07689, 2110.09150) confirms score-shift and EER degradation is a known, architecture-dependent phenomenon across language pairs, and is mitigated by language-aware calibration — not by better embeddings alone.
- **Bottom line**: expect meaningfully worse (roughly 5-10x) EER on Vietnamese speech from an English-VoxCeleb-only embedding model vs its VoxCeleb1-O headline number. A VoxCeleb1-O EER of 0.7% is not indicative of in-app accuracy; budget for something closer to mid-single-digit-percent EER at best, likely worse given also-short, also-far-field conditions stack on top (see §3, §5).

## 3. Utterance duration vs accuracy — the load-bearing question

Concrete numbers found:

- General trend (multiple sources, e.g. arXiv 2011.04896, 2606.16115): EER falls continuously as duration grows from 1s→10s; below ~3-4s degradation is steep, and below 2s it's severe.
- One directly-cited data point: shortening eval utterances from 3.59s → 2.05s raised EER from 8.72% → 12.8% (**+46% relative**) in one text-independent SV study.
- Best concrete curve found: "Stabilizing Short Duration Speaker Verification" (arXiv 2606.16115, VoxPhrase corpus, CAM++ backbone):
  - Text-independent (TI) enrollment, 3s test: **8.86% EER**
  - TI enrollment, 10s test: **~2.03% EER**
  - Text-dependent (TD, phrase-based) enrollment 0.8-3s: **3.62% EER** (TD beats TI specifically because it's phrase-matched, not applicable to your free-speech scenario)
  - Their finding explicitly: "when T<2s, TI remains inferior to TD" — i.e. plain free-speech embeddings under 2s are the worst regime, consistent with the general trend.
- **Implication for this app**: turn-level audio in a live conversation is very frequently <3s (single sentence/phrase before a VAD hangover cuts). Expect single-turn speaker-embedding EER well above the 8-13% range even in matched-language, close-talk conditions — before you add cross-lingual Vietnamese penalty (§2) and far-field penalty (§5), which compound. This is a real risk to "accurate" as a requirement for short turns, not just a tuning problem.
- Practical mitigation used in the field: accumulate/average embeddings across a speaker's turns over the session (running centroid) rather than trusting single-turn embeddings — turns get more reliable as more speech accumulates per speaker, at the cost of early-session mislabels.

## 4. Online diarization with unknown speaker count

- No single agreed SOTA; approaches: incremental/constrained agglomerative clustering (compare new local embedding against running per-speaker centroids; spawn new cluster if best-match distance exceeds a tuned threshold), UIS-RNN (distance-dependent Chinese Restaurant Process — fully supervised, learns cluster/no-cluster switching, **7.6% DER on NIST SRE2000 CALLHOME**, offline-trained but usable online), VBx (two-stage Bayesian HMM clustering — SOTA-tier accuracy but explicitly **not designed for online/streaming** use per its own authors), online spectral clustering, LS-EEND / online-attractor end-to-end neural diarization (2024-2025 work, arXiv 2410.06670) which learns to emit new speaker attractors on the fly without a fixed max-speaker count.
- On-device/CPU-specific: arXiv 2606.08505 ("Fast and Robust On-Device Speaker Diarization: Relative Minimum Cluster Size for Stride-Accelerated Pipelines") is the closest analog to your exact constraints (streaming, CPU, no GPU). Their key idea: instead of a fixed distance threshold for "is this a new speaker," they scale the minimum-cluster-size requirement relative to the current clustering state before confirming a new cluster — reduces false new-speaker spawns from noisy short segments. I could not extract concrete DER/RTF numbers from the PDF fetch (compressed/malformed text extraction); worth a manual read of the actual PDF before committing to this approach — flagging as unresolved.
- **Threshold calibration**: all incremental methods reduce to "is distance(new_embedding, existing_centroids) > τ" with τ tuned per-corpus; none of the sources found publish an EER-optimal Vietnamese/far-field/short-turn τ — you would need to calibrate this empirically on your own room/mic setup, there is no published number to copy.
- **Realistic DER for far-field single-mic conversational, unknown-N**: no source gave a single number that matches your exact conditions (far-field + single mic + streaming + unknown N + short turns simultaneously). Closest proxies: AMI SDM (single distant mic, offline, known/bounded N) sits around 18-37% DER depending on system/era (see §5); that is a floor, not your number — online + unknown-N + short-turn will be worse.

## 5. Far-field / single-mic degradation

- ECAPA-TDNN system comparison (oracle #speakers, arXiv 2104.01466): HeadsetMix (close-talk) **2.02% dev / 1.78% eval** DER vs distant-mic (far-field) **2.81% dev / 3.12% eval** DER — this particular well-engineered system shows the far-field penalty is real but not catastrophic (~1.3-1.7x) _when_ the system already includes far-field-robust segmentation/beamforming-quality training. That's a best-case, curated system.
- AMI corpus, more representative of casual/production setups: SDM (single distant mic) diarization reported around **37.2% DER including overlap** in one older system (2012-era published number, not current SOTA) vs low single digits with headsets. Modern pyannote-class systems on AMI SDM land closer to the **12-19% DER** range per current pyannote 3.1 benchmark tables (AMI ~12-14%, DIHARD III ~17-19%, VoxConverse ~9-11%) — but note pyannote's AMI number is typically reported on the array-mix, and single-distant-mic-only conditions run several points worse than array-mix.
- **Practical read**: a single laptop/phone mic in a room, no array, no beamforming, is closer to the harder end of that range (teens-to-20s% DER) even with a strong offline system; expect worse in a real-time/online/unknown-N setting.

## 6. Practical alternatives alongside sherpa-onnx

- **pyannote.audio 3.1**: MIT-licensed code, but the actual pretrained pipeline weights (`pyannote/speaker-diarization-3.1`, `pyannote/segmentation-3.0`) are **gated on HuggingFace** — free to use but requires accepting a click-through user agreement per-model and (for the pipeline) providing an HF token at runtime; not a pure offline/no-network drop-in unless you pre-download and self-host the weights. Needs **PyTorch**, not onnxruntime — v3.1 removed onnxruntime dependency entirely, so no ABI conflict risk with your pinned onnxruntime 1.27.0, but it adds a whole separate ML runtime (torch) to your sidecar image, and reported CPU RTF is poor (~0.5-1x RTF meaning near-real-time-to-slower-than-real-time on CPU per a GitHub issue — i.e. a 1-hour file can take 30-60 min on CPU). That's likely too slow for your per-turn low-latency budget unless run as short-clip embedding-only (skip their full pipeline, just use segmentation/embedding models directly), which is more integration work.
- **NeMo diarization**: also PyTorch-based (same TitaNet backbone as the ONNX export you already have access to), heavier dependency footprint than needed if you're already extracting TitaNet via sherpa-onnx's ONNX export — no compelling reason to bring in the full NeMo runtime just for diarization when the embedding model is already available via ONNX.
- **WeSpeaker's own runtime**: has a C++/onnxruntime runtime path too, but sherpa-onnx already re-exports the WeSpeaker ONNX models with its own extractor API — using WeSpeaker's native runtime instead of sherpa-onnx's wrapper would just be a redundant integration for no accuracy gain (same weights).
- **Silero VAD / ten-vad**: not diarization tools, but relevant since you're using a homegrown RMS-VAD — TEN VAD (Apache-2.0, sherpa-onnx already exposes `TenVadModelConfig`) claims better precision/recall than Silero VAD and much lower latency (RTF ~0.015, 306KB lib) per TEN's own docs (self-reported, not independently verified in this search); Silero VAD is reported to have "several hundred ms" detection delay in some comparisons. If turn-boundary precision matters for speaker attribution (a badly-placed boundary bleeds one speaker's tail into another's turn), swapping your RMS-VAD for TEN VAD (already available in your stack) is a low-risk, low-effort accuracy lever worth considering — separate from the embedding-model choice.
- **Recommendation given constraints**: stay entirely inside sherpa-onnx (CAM++ or WeSpeaker ResNet34-LM embedding, English/VoxCeleb, smallest/fastest options given the accuracy near-parity in §1) plus your own incremental-clustering logic for unknown-N. Pulling in pyannote or NeMo adds a second heavy ML runtime (torch) for marginal-to-negative latency trade at your real-time budget, and the pyannote weights being HF-gated adds a licensing/ops step you don't currently need. If accuracy at short-turn/far-field/Vietnamese proves unacceptable in testing, the pyannote _segmentation_ model alone (not full pipeline) is the more promising piece to evaluate before the diarization pipeline as a whole.

## 7. Known failure modes (production diarization)

- **Speaker collapse**: multiple real speakers get merged into one cluster, typically from over-permissive thresholds or when speakers' voices are acoustically similar (same gender/age range) — worse under far-field + short-turn conditions where embeddings are already noisier.
- **Same speaker split across clusters**: the inverse failure — a single speaker gets fragmented into 2+ clusters, common when channel/gain/mic-distance shifts mid-session (person moves, background noise changes) shift their embedding enough to cross the new-speaker threshold.
- **Drift over a long session**: clustering decisions made early (when few reference embeddings exist per speaker) are less reliable; centroids can drift as noisy short-turn embeddings get averaged in, especially problematic for an incremental/online system without a periodic re-clustering/cleanup pass.
- **Channel/gain sensitivity**: compression, automatic gain control, or noise suppression on the capture path can measurably shift embeddings mid-session and trick the system into spawning spurious "new speakers." Relevant because a browser-mic → sidecar path often has AGC/noise-suppression toggled by the browser itself, outside your control unless explicitly disabled via `getUserMedia` constraints.
- **VAD-cut mid-word**: if your 500ms-hangover RMS-VAD clips a turn mid-utterance, the resulting short fragment both (a) has worse per-turn EER per §3, and (b) can look acoustically odd (missing onset/offset phones), a double-hit on attribution accuracy for exactly the short-turn case that's already your worst-case regime.
- **Overlapping speech**: not explicitly asked about but is the other standard failure mode in every source found — a single-mic energy-RMS VAD has no way to detect or separate overlapping speakers; segments with two people talking will get assigned to whichever speaker's embedding dominates, silently misattributing part of the turn.
